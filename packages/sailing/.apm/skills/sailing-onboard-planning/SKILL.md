# Sailing On-board Planning

The skipper is responsible for the crew and the boat, and the crew documents carry that responsibility. This skill sets what each document contains and how a draft gets agreed. Layout, design and tone come from the skipper's existing documents, not from here.

## Before drafting anything

1. **Look for what already exists.** Check the project files, uploads and earlier documents for the skipper's existing crew documents. Never say a document doesn't exist after searching chat history alone.
2. **Confirm the boat and charter facts the document depends on.** These include cabins and heads, storage in cabins, galley and fridge size, oven, water and shore power, and what the charter includes (bedding, towels, dinghy, outboard). Also confirm what the contract forbids, such as night sailing. A wrong assumption here goes out to every crew member.
3. **Know the crew's shape**: couples and singles, sailing experience, diets, whether they all know each other, who arrives by car and who flies, and any crew change mid-trip.
4. **Derive the weather yourself.** Work out season, sea and night temperatures and the typical winds from the dates and the place with a search. Don't ask the skipper for it.

## Agreeing a document with the skipper

1. **Draft over-complete.** Number every item, and mark the ones the skipper might supply centrally. Expect heavy pruning; that is the point of the draft.
2. **Hand over an annotatable file before any layout.** One numbered table with a comment column, which the skipper answers inline or as "number: verdict".
3. **Apply every review comment in the same turn.** Make the edit, confirm it in one line, and remove the point from the open list. Never return an empty reply to a review. If a comment raises a new question, ask it once.
4. **Finalise the content.** Drop the comment column, and keep a hint only where it states a reason or a constraint.

Content rules that hold for every crew document:

- **Label each item**: mandatory, optional, if you have one, or shareable per couple or cabin.
- **Central gear appears twice**: on the captain list, and in an "already aboard, don't bring" box on the crew list.
- **Each fact lives in one document.** Insurance, emergency contacts, health and travel data belong in the survey, not in packing lists. Boat-fund mechanics stay out unless the skipper asks for them.
- **Every person-specific line traces to a survey answer**, such as who offered to cook what or who is prone to seasickness. Refer to crew by survey number, not by name, in anything shared beyond the boat.

## Gotchas

- **At takeover, dive under the boat at the first chance** to check hull, keels or saildrives, rudders and propellers. **Film everything** — inside, deck, rig and every existing defect — before leaving the base. The video is the evidence at check-out.
- **Size clothing to the boat's daily rhythm, not a hotel holiday.** Ask how the skipper runs the day (when people shower, what they wear sailing versus going ashore) and derive quantities from it.
- **Heavy electrical loads such as hair dryers work only on shore power.** Say which nights are on a berth before the crew packs for them.
- **Crew lists and certificates have a charter deadline before boarding.** Find it in the booking or the charter company's instructions and plan the survey backwards from it. Don't assume a lead time.
- **Payment and phone habits don't travel.** Check whether the crew's usual mobile payment app and roaming plan work in the destination, and how much cash small ports and buoy fields need.
- **Personal data collected for the crew list** (ID numbers, dates of birth, health, emergency contacts) is deleted after the trip, as the survey promised.
