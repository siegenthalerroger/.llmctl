# Deck Structures

A deck needs one narrative structure, chosen from what the audience is being asked to do. Picking the structure is a decision; defaulting to chronology is what happens when nobody picks.

## Choosing a structure

Start from the ask, not the material:

| The audience must | Structure |
|---|---|
| Accept a conclusion they have not considered | Situation → complication → question → answer |
| Change something they are invested in | Current state versus possible state |
| Approve a fix for something already agreed to be broken | Problem → solution → proof |
| Choose between real alternatives | Options and recommendation |
| Understand how something unfolded | Chronological |

**When more than one fits, pick the one with the shortest path to the answer.** Structure exists to get the audience to the conclusion with the least held in memory.

## Situation, complication, question, answer

The default for an argument the audience has not already accepted.

- **Situation** — what everyone in the room already agrees is true. Establish common ground, briefly. If this takes more than a slide or two, the audience is not as aligned as the deck assumes.
- **Complication** — what has changed or what breaks that picture. This is where the tension enters, and where the audience starts caring.
- **Question** — the question the complication forces. Often unstated on a slide but it must be answerable in one sentence.
- **Answer** — your conclusion, stated plainly, then supported.

**Put the answer early, not at the end.** Withholding it turns the audience into detectives and costs their attention on every supporting slide.

## Current state versus possible state

The default for persuasion, where the ask is behavioural rather than analytical.

Alternate deliberately between what is true now and what could be true, returning to the contrast several times. The gap between the two states is the engine — each oscillation makes it more concrete and harder to be comfortable with.

- Open in the current state, so the audience recognises their own situation.
- End in the possible state, made specific enough to be believed.
- **The audience is the one who acts.** The deck's job is to make the change appear both necessary and achievable to *them*, not to make the presenter appear right.

Use where the barrier is willingness. Do not use where the barrier is evidence — a sceptical technical audience wants the pyramid, not the oscillation.

## Problem, solution, proof

For an audience that already agrees something is wrong.

Skip the persuasion about the problem existing — one slide restating it in their own terms is enough — and spend the deck on whether *this* solution works. Most of the weight goes on proof: evidence, precedent, cost, risk, and what happens if it fails.

The common failure is spending half the deck re-establishing a problem the audience raised in the first place.

## Options and recommendation

For a genuine decision between real alternatives.

- **State the recommendation first**, then the options, then why the others lose. Presenting options neutrally and recommending at the end invites the audience to relitigate.
- **Use the same criteria for every option**, and say what the criteria are before applying them. Different criteria per option is how a deck smuggles in a predetermined answer, and audiences notice.
- **Include the option of doing nothing** where it is real. Omitting it makes the deck look like advocacy rather than analysis.
- **Do not present options you would refuse to implement.** Straw options destroy trust in the ones that remain.

## Chronological

Legitimate for a post-mortem, an incident review, or a history where sequence carries the causation.

**It is illegitimate everywhere else**, and it is the structure a deck falls into when nobody chose one — because chronology is how the work happened, not how the argument runs. The audience does not need the order in which you learned things.

If chronology is genuinely right, still put the conclusion first and use the sequence as the support.

## Grouping the support

Whatever the structure, the support beneath each claim follows two rules:

- **Each group actually supports the claim above it.** Read a claim, then its supporting points, and ask whether the points establish the claim or merely relate to it. Related-but-not-supporting is the most common structural defect in a deck.
- **The groups do not overlap, and together they are sufficient.** Overlapping branches make the argument feel repetitive; a gap is what an audience finds when they ask the question the deck did not anticipate.

Three or four supporting branches per claim is the practical ceiling. More than that usually means the branches belong at a lower level, or that the claim above them is doing too much.
